<candidate_response>I'm sorry — this sounds like a genuinely painful few weeks. You arrived not knowing anyone, you took real risks by asking two people to lunch, and both said no. Then you sat beside a conversation you couldn't decode and left after ten minutes. That's a lot of small rejections stacked on top of each other, and it makes sense that you'd start protecting yourself.

I want to be honest with you about one thing, though, because I think it matters more than reassurance: the conclusion "they obviously don't want me around" is doing a lot of work from very little evidence.

**What the evidence actually supports:**
- Two people said they were busy on the day you asked. "Busy" is genuinely the most common reason people decline — especially in the first weeks of term, when everyone is drowning in new schedules.
- One of them still sends you memes. That is not nothing. People don't usually maintain casual contact with someone they're trying to avoid — and it's also not proof of friendship. It's a small open door.
- At the club, you couldn't tell whether the Saturday plans were club-wide or private. That's a real ambiguity, not a rejection. Private plans among people who already know each other are normal and aren't aimed at you.

**What isn't evidence at all:** that they "already have friends, easy for them." You're seeing the output of months or years of accumulated contact, not the effort. And "if I stop trying I won't look desperate" — pulling back does protect you from the sting of asking. It also guarantees the outcome you're afraid of.

**One thing to try this week — small and low-risk:**

Send the meme-sender one message that doesn't ask for anything. Something like:

> "Hey, thanks for the memes — they genuinely make my day. Random question: are you doing anything for the club this weekend? I'm still figuring out what's actually happening with club stuff vs. people's own plans."

This does three things: it's warm without being a request, it asks a real informational question (which is easy to answer), and it clears up the ambiguity that confused you at the gathering. If they reply with something, you have a conversation. If they don't, you've lost almost nothing.

**On the group chat:** you don't have to leave messages unread as a strategy. You can just... participate normally when something interests you, and not monitor whether people respond. The "waiting to be noticed" feeling is real, but it's a reason to lower the stakes of each message, not to opt out of the chat entirely.

**On whether to go back to the club:** I wouldn't decide that while you're feeling raw. One more visit, with a different plan — arrive with one small goal, like asking one person a question about something concrete (what they're studying, how they found the club) rather than hoping to be invited into a group — is a much better test than one ten-minute sit.
