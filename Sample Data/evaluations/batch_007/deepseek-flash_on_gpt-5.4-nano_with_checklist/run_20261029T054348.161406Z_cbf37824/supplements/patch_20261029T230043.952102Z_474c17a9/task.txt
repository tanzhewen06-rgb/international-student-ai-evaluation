<candidate_response>That sounds really painful—especially when you’re new here and you’re already working hard to manage language, classes, and unfamiliar social norms. I also hear you getting pulled toward a “protect myself” conclusion (“they don’t want me around… I shouldn’t keep trying”), but the evidence you described doesn’t actually prove that. It might be rejection, it might be timing/busyness, and it might also be confusion because you’re new and not yet “visible” in their social circle.

A few important reality-checks:

- **“Busy” isn’t the same as “doesn’t want you.”** People can be avoiding plans, dealing with their own schedule, or just not knowing how to respond to a new person.
- **Memes sometimes + not outright blocking you** suggests at least some openness (even if it’s limited). That’s not the behavior of someone who “obviously” doesn’t want you around.
- **At the club gathering, them talking plans near you** could mean they assumed you weren’t interested, they forgot to include you, or they didn’t realize you were waiting for an invitation. In groups, people often don’t notice who’s standing there quietly.

And about the group chat: leaving messages unread because you feel “stupid” is a very understandable feeling—but it also creates a loop where you get *less* social evidence that you’re being included.

### So—should you stop going to the club?
I wouldn’t jump to “there’s no point.” That’s too final given you’ve only had a couple awkward moments and you’re still early in the semester. A better strategy is: **change the way you approach**, not stop completely.

### A workable next step (low embarrassment, high clarity)
For the next club meeting or the next time you see someone, try a **direct but easy** invite that removes the ambiguity:

**Script (short, friendly):**
> “Hey, I’m new here—are you going to anything this Saturday? If you’re free, I’d love to join for lunch or coffee.”

Why this helps:
- It’s not asking them to “include you” in a big group way (which can feel intimidating).
- It gives them a clear yes/no.
- It replaces guesswork with a real invitation.

If that feels too bold, try a softer version:
> “Do you know anyone going to [place/time] this Saturday? I’m trying to make plans.”

That gives people an easy entry to reply, and it’s socially “safe” even if they don’t respond immediately.
